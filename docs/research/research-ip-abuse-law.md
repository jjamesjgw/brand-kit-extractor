# Research: IP, content, abuse, and terms-of-service law

Scope: the brand kit tool hosted on a subdomain of wethejamesphoto.com, operated by Justin James (individual, Rowlett, Texas). Research date 2026-11-08. This is product research, not legal advice; section 4 lists what a licensed attorney should review before launch.

Confidence tags: **[Certain]** = primary source read or settled black-letter law. **[Likely]** = strong inference or reliable secondary source. **[Guessing]** = design judgment or gap-filling.

The tool's name is not decided. `{Tool}` stands for the product name and `{host}` for its hostname (for example `kit.wethejamesphoto.com`).

---

## 0. Conflicts with locked decisions (read first)

These do not reopen any decision. Each one is a place where a locked decision and a legal or abuse risk meet, so the owner should see it before building.

1. **Share links on the wedding business's domain.** Google Safe Browsing strips the `#fragment` before matching. It then checks the exact host plus parent hosts, so `wethejamesphoto.com/` is one of the expressions looked up for every page on the tool's subdomain. One phishing kit reported to Google could get every share link flagged. In the worst case, a listing at the registrable-domain level would also flag the photography site. [Certain on the matching rules; Guessing on how Google would scope a listing] Source: https://developers.google.com/safe-browsing/v4/urls-hashing. Mitigations are in S-1 and S-2. The strongest option is to serve the share viewer from a separate free hostname, such as the host's default `*.pages.dev` name. That keeps the wedding domain out of the blast radius, but it departs from "subdomain of wethejamesphoto.com" for that one route. **Owner decision.**
2. **DMCA designated agent costs $6 for each 3-year period.** That breaks the "$0 forever" rule. As designed (nothing stored), the tool does not need the §512(c) safe harbor, so **do not register**. If kits are ever stored server-side, revisit. [Likely] See §2.6.
3. **Any server-side storage of kits (short links, "save my kit", galleries) changes the legal category of the product.** Under the EU Digital Services Act, storage makes it a *hosting service*. A non-EU provider of any intermediary service must appoint a paid EU legal representative (Art. 13), and a hosting service also needs notice-and-action (Art. 16). Those costs break the $0 rule. **Keep share data fragment-only, permanently.** [Likely] See §2.6.
4. **"No data capture" vs. a report channel.** Any report channel receives something. A `mailto:` link means the owner receives the reporter's email address, and that reporter could be a child. R-1 and R-2 give two options and how to handle each. **Owner decision.**
5. **UK Online Safety Act.** Share links might make the tool a "user-to-user service", because content one user generates on the service can be "encountered" by another user on the service. Storage does not matter under that test. The obligations for a small, low-risk service are paperwork, not money, but they are real. [Guessing on whether it applies] See §2.6.
6. **Parity with the competitor's "fetch any site".** The safe fetcher policy (honor robots.txt, an honest User-Agent, never bypass bot walls) means some sites will refuse the fetch. The product needs a first-class fallback ("upload a screenshot or logo instead"), which inputs (b)–(e) already provide. [Likely]

## 1. Concrete requirements for this product

Each item gives the requirement, the reason, a source, and a confidence tag. IDs are for cross-reference from the spec.

### F. Fetcher (URL → extraction)

- **F-1 Fetch only what one page needs.** Fetch the submitted URL, the stylesheets it links (including `@import`, depth ≤ 3), and the icon/manifest/logo candidates it references. Do not follow `<a>` links, do not crawl, do not run JavaScript, and do not submit forms. Suggested caps: ≤ 20 subrequests per analysis, HTML ≤ 2 MB, each CSS file ≤ 1 MB, total ≤ 5 MB, ≤ 5 images at ≤ 1 MB each, 10 s overall timeout, ≤ 5 redirects with every hop re-validated. *Why:* recent scraping claims that survived dismissal rest on server burden or "impairment" (X Corp. v. Bright Data, amended claims allowed late 2024). A small, bounded footprint defeats any harm element. The trespass-to-chattels line (Intel v. Hamidi, Cal. 2003) also requires actual harm. *Sources:* https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/05/district-court-adopts-broad-view ; https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2025/01/catching-up-on-the-heavyweight-scraping-battle-between-x-and-bright-data-guest-blog-post.htm . [Certain that the claims survived on an impairment theory; Guessing on the exact caps, which should be reconciled with hosting limits]
- **F-2 Fetch only on an explicit request from a person who passed the human check.** No background, scheduled, or pre-fetching. *Why:* this is what keeps the tool a "user-triggered fetcher", the category Google uses for its own on-demand fetchers. *Source:* https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-user-triggered-fetchers . [Certain on Google's category]
- **F-3 Honest User-Agent; never spoof a browser.** Send:
  `User-Agent: Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; {Token}/1.0; +https://{host}/bot)`
  `{Token}` is the robots.txt product token. Per RFC 9309 §2.2.1 it may contain **only letters A–Z/a–z, `_` and `-` (no digits, no spaces)**, for example `Swatch-Fetcher`. It should also appear as a substring of the UA string. Optionally send `From: <dedicated bot-contact address>`; RFC 9110 says robotic agents SHOULD send one. *Why:* (1) Texas Penal Code §33.01(12)(A) says consent "is not effective if … induced by deception". A disguised UA is the most obvious deception a site owner could plead under Penal Code §33.02 and the civil action in Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code ch. 143. (2) It lets sites opt out with robots.txt. *Sources:* https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309.html ; https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.33.htm ; https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/CP/htm/CP.143.htm ; https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#field.from . [Certain on RFC 9309 token rules and Texas statute text; Likely on the From header SHOULD]
- **F-4 Honor robots.txt per RFC 9309, for every origin fetched.** Fetch `/robots.txt` once per origin per analysis. Obey the group that matches `{Token}` case-insensitively, else the `*` group. A 4xx response means no restrictions. A 5xx response or network error means treat the origin as fully disallowed. Parse at least 500 KiB. Follow up to 5 redirects. If the page URL is disallowed, stop and show: *"This site asks automated tools not to read this page. Upload your logo, a screenshot, or a brand PDF instead."* If only some stylesheets are disallowed, skip them and say so in the results. An in-memory cache of up to 24 h is allowed by the RFC, but per-request is simpler and avoids storage. *Why:* robots.txt is not legally required in the US, and RFC 9309 itself says "These rules are not a form of access authorization." Honoring it is still cheap evidence of good faith. It is also the leading machine-readable opt-out under the EU text-and-data-mining exception (DSM Directive Art. 4(3); OLG Hamburg, *Kneschke v. LAION*, 10 Dec 2025, stressed machine-readability). Finally, it keeps the tool out of the "knew and kept going" fact pattern that made browsewrap terms binding in *Southwest v. Kiwi* (N.D. Tex. 2021). Google's own user-triggered fetchers "generally ignore robots.txt", so this policy is stricter than industry practice by choice. *Sources:* https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309.html ; https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/790/oj ; https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/germany/higher-regional-court-hamburg-confirms-ai-training-was-permitted-(kneschke-v,-d-,-laion) ; https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-user-triggered-fetchers . [Certain on RFC text; Likely on the legal benefit; Guessing that the stricter policy is worth the lost fetches]
- **F-5 Never get past a gate.** On 401/403/429, a CAPTCHA or challenge page, a login wall, a paywall, or a geo/IP block: stop and offer the upload fallback. Do not retry with another UA, IP, proxy, headless browser, or cookies. Never send a forged `Referer` or `Origin`. *Why:* *Van Buren v. United States*, 593 U.S. 374 (2021) adopted a "gates-up-or-down" test but left open (fn. 8) whether contractual or only technical limits count. *hiQ v. LinkedIn*, 31 F.4th 1180 (9th Cir. 2022) held that "without authorization" doesn't reach public pages. Getting around a technical barrier is exactly where CFAA and Texas ch. 33 exposure comes back. Forged referrers to pull domain-locked fonts would also be circumvention under 17 U.S.C. §1201 (see T-4). *Sources:* https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-783_k53l.pdf ; https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/12/08/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case/ ; https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1201 . [Certain on holdings; Likely on application]
- **F-6 Keep a do-not-fetch list, in the repo, honored before any request.** It is a static JSON of hostnames whose owners asked not to be fetched. Add a domain within 2 business days of any cease-and-desist or request, no argument needed. *Why:* in *Southwest Airlines v. Kiwi.com* (N.D. Tex., Sept. 30, 2021; later a permanent injunction after Kiwi dropped its Fifth Circuit appeal), a court in the owner's home district held that continuing to use a site after a cease-and-desist letter supplies actual knowledge, which makes browsewrap terms enforceable. *Sources:* https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2021/10/tos-supports-injunction-against-web-scraping-southwest-airlines-v-kiwi.htm ; https://manage.lawstreetmedia.com/?p=15231 . [Certain on the holding; Likely on its relevance here]
- **F-7 Publish a `/bot` page** (linked from the UA). It should say: what the fetcher reads (one page plus its CSS, icons, and logo files), when (only when a person pastes that URL), the caps from F-1, that nothing fetched is stored, how to block it (a `User-agent: {Token}` / `Disallow: /` example), and how to get on the do-not-fetch list (email). Note that requests come from the host's network; on Cloudflare Workers every subrequest carries a `CF-Worker: <zone>` header naming the owning zone. *Why:* transparency and an easy opt-out remove the motive to sue. *Source:* https://developers.cloudflare.com/fundamentals/reference/http-headers/ . [Certain on the CF-Worker header; Likely on the benefit]
- **F-8 Per-target and per-client throttles.** For example, ≤ 30 analyses per target origin per hour across all users, and per-client limits behind the human check. Block private, loopback, link-local, and metadata IP ranges and non-80/443 ports, and re-check on every redirect (SSRF). *Why:* stops the tool being used to hammer one site (the server-burden theory) or to probe internal networks. [Likely]
- **F-9 Store nothing and log nothing that was fetched.** Return the extracted facts, plus any image bytes or CSS the client needs, then discard. Never write fetched HTML, CSS, or images to KV, caches, or logs. If raw HTML/CSS goes back to the browser, parse it as inert text (DOMParser or a CSS parser). Never inject it into the app's origin. [Likely]
- **F-10 Optional, near-free: honor TDMRep.** If a fetched response carries `tdm-reservation: 1`, treat the page as opted out, same as F-4. It needs no extra request when only the header is checked. *Source:* https://www.w3.org/community/reports/tdmrep/CG-FINAL-tdmrep-20220216/ . [Guessing that it's worth doing; Certain on the header name]

### E. Extraction output and labeling

- **E-1 Show only facts plus provenance.** Hex/RGB values, font-family names, the detected load source, brand name and tagline from metadata, and logo candidates, with the source URL as **plain text, not a link**. Never reproduce the site's stylesheet or copy. *Why:* colors and names are not copyrightable. 37 C.F.R. §202.1(a) excludes "names, titles, and slogans; familiar symbols or designs; mere variations of … coloring" and §202.1(e) excludes "typeface as typeface". Facts are free to copy (*Feist v. Rural*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991)). CSS *code* can be protectable expression, so don't republish it. *Source:* https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-37/chapter-II/subchapter-A/part-202/section-202.1 . [Certain]
- **E-2 Do not use "approved" or "official" anywhere in generated kits.** Rename "approved text-on-background pairs" to **"pairs that pass WCAG AA"** (and "…AAA"). Title kits "Brand kit", never "Official brand guidelines". *Why:* "approved" implies the brand owner approved it, which is a false endorsement risk under Lanham Act §43(a) and deceptive in a shared link. [Likely]
- **E-3 Label generated logo rules as suggestions.** On the logo-usage page: *"Starter rules computed from this logo's proportions by {Tool}. Not the brand owner's rules."* [Likely]
- **E-4 Describe the contrast checker accurately.** *"Contrast ratios use the WCAG 2.2 formula (4.5:1 AA and 7:1 AAA for body text; 3:1 AA and 4.5:1 AAA for large text). Passing these checks is not a full accessibility review."* *Why:* users may rely on it for ADA or EU Accessibility Act work, and an overclaim is an FTC Act §5 and warranty risk. *Source:* https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ . [Certain on the thresholds; Likely on the risk]
- **E-5 Describe font license labels accurately.** *"Font sources are detected from how the site loads them. They are not a license check."* [Likely]

### T. Fonts (rendering and embedding)

- **T-1 Embed only open-licensed font binaries from an open-license distribution.** Allowed sources: Google Fonts (the CSS API or the `google/fonts` GitHub repo), Fontsource, or Bunny Fonts, matched by family name. Allowed licenses: SIL OFL 1.1, Apache-2.0, Ubuntu Font Licence. **Never download font binaries from the scanned site**, even when they look like an OFL family, because a site's copy may be modified, subset, or commercially licensed. *Sources:* Google Fonts FAQ: "Yes, you can use them commercially … The most common license is the SIL Open Font License … Some fonts are under the Apache license or Ubuntu Font License" and "Yes, you can use the fonts on any surface" (https://developers.google.com/fonts/faq). [Certain]
- **T-2 OFL embedding in PDFs is permitted, full or subset.** OFL-FAQ 1.12: "Yes, either in full or a subset. The restrictions regarding font modification and redistribution do not apply, as the font is not intended for use outside the document." 1.13: embedding "does not change the license of the document itself … creating any kind of graphic using a font under the OFL does not make the resulting artwork subject to the OFL." 1.10: the license text need not accompany a font embedded in a document. *Source:* https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/ (OFL-FAQ 1.1-update7, Nov 2023). [Certain]
- **T-3 Check OS/2 `fsType` before embedding any font.** 0 or 8 → embed (subsetting OK). 4 (Preview & Print) → embed. 2 (Restricted) → do not embed, use the T-5 fallback. Bit 0x0100 (no subsetting) → embed the whole font. Bit 0x0200 (bitmap only) → do not embed outlines, use the fallback. Under OS/2 v0–v2, if several of bits 1–3 are set, the least restrictive wins. *Source:* https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/opentype/spec/os2 . [Certain]
- **T-4 Never fetch or embed Adobe Fonts, Monotype/Fonts.com, Hoefler/Typography.com, MyFonts, or other commercial web fonts.** Detect them only, by host: `use.typekit.net` / `p.typekit.net` = Adobe Fonts; `fast.fonts.net` = Monotype; `cloud.typography.com` = Hoefler&Co. Same-origin or unknown CDN = "self-hosted, license unknown". *Why:* the Adobe Fonts Product Specific Terms (2024-06-18) license only the *subscriber*, "only for as long as you maintain an uninterrupted Adobe Subscription Plan". They prohibit "hosting the Licensed Content on your own server", distributing it "in any way that would allow another person to use the Licensed Fonts", and "attempting to … access or use the Licensed Fonts other than by subscribing directly". The tool has no license at all. Kits are also domain-locked, so pulling them would mean defeating a protection mechanism (also prohibited, §3.4(E)(5)) and would risk 17 U.S.C. §1201. *Source:* https://wwwimages2.adobe.com/content/dam/cc/en/legal/servicetou/Adobe-Fonts-Product-Specific-Terms-en_US-20240618.pdf . [Certain on contract text; Likely on §1201]
- **T-5 Substitute and label.** When the brand font can't be embedded, render specimens with an open-licensed substitute and say so wherever the font appears (PDF, PNG, UI, share view):
  > *Brand font: **Gotham** (loaded from a commercial service; not included). Shown in **Montserrat** (SIL OFL 1.1), a free substitute. License Gotham from its foundry to use it.*
  Keep a curated, versioned mapping in the repo. Prefer metric-compatible open fonts for common system fonts: Arial → Arimo, Times New Roman → Tinos, Courier New → Cousine, Calibri → Carlito, Cambria → Caladea, Georgia → Gelasio. Fall back by classification (geometric sans, humanist sans, transitional serif, slab, mono, script). Never call a substitute "identical" or "the same". *Why:* font names such as Helvetica and Gotham are trademarks. Naming them to identify the font is nominative use, but implying the substitute *is* the brand font misleads. [Likely; the specific pairings are Guessing and need a designer's review]
- **T-6 Self-hosted OFL fonts must ship with their license.** If the tool self-hosts any open font files (for its UI or for embedding), place `OFL.txt` (or the Apache/UFL text) and the copyright notice beside them and list each one on the credits page. OFL-FAQ 1.8 says hosting fonts for others is fine "as long as you make the full font package available". *Source:* https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/ . [Certain]
- **T-7 Do not "optimize" or rename fonts for the UI without checking Reserved Font Names.** OFL-FAQ 2.6: subsetting a *webfont* is modification and "would not normally allow the use of RFNs". PDF embedding is exempt (T-2), so this only matters for webfonts the tool serves. Easiest path: serve upstream WOFF2 files unchanged. [Certain on FAQ; Likely on the practical advice]
- **T-8 Where font files load from matters for privacy (cross-reference).** Loading Google Fonts from Google's servers sends the visitor's IP to Google. LG München I (20 Jan 2022, 3 O 17493/20) awarded €100 for that without consent. Self-hosting, Fontsource-via-own-origin, or Bunny Fonts (which states a zero-logging policy) avoids it. This belongs to the privacy research track; flagged because it affects font sourcing. *Sources:* https://www.gesetze-bayern.de/Content/Document/Y-300-Z-GRURRS-B-2022-N-612 ; https://bunny.net/blog/bringing-privacy-back-into-your-own-hands-introducing-bunny-fonts/ . [Certain on the ruling; Likely on Bunny's claims, which are the vendor's own]
- **T-9 PNG exports need no font license step.** Rasterized text is an image of text, not a font. Google Fonts FAQ: text in images "isn't another font, but an image of text"; OFL-FAQ 1.13 agrees. [Certain]

### L. Logos and trademarks inside kits

- **L-1 A third-party logo appears only where the user put it.** It comes from the page the user submitted or from the user's own upload. The tool never suggests, searches for, or pre-fills other brands' logos. [Likely]
- **L-2 Provenance line on every PDF page footer and on the PNG board** (small, not removable in the UI): *"Made with {Tool} · {YYYY-MM-DD} · Source: {domain or 'user uploads'} · Not verified by the brand owner."* **Colophon (last PDF page):**
  > *Made with {Tool} ({host}) on {date}. Sources: {domain}, fetched {date}; files supplied by the person who made this kit. Brand names, logos, and fonts belong to their owners. {Tool} and its maker are not affiliated with or endorsed by {Brand}, and did not check who made this kit. Fonts marked "substitute" are free stand-ins; only open-licensed fonts are embedded.*
  *Why:* nominative fair use (*New Kids on the Block*, 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992); adopted in modified form by the Fifth Circuit in *Pebble Beach v. Tour 18*, 155 F.3d 526 (5th Cir. 1998)) turns on not suggesting sponsorship. *Pebble Beach* also noted that disclaimers did not cure confusion there, so the disclaimer supplements the design choices in S-*; it does not replace them. *Source:* https://nick-guinn.squarespace.com/blog/we-always-beat-xs-prices-a-look-at-nominative-fair-use-in-the-fifth-circuit (secondary). [Certain on the doctrine; Likely on application]
- **L-3 User representation at the point of risk.**
  - *Next to the primary action (notice, no checkbox):* "By using {Tool} you agree to the [Terms]. Build kits for brands you own or have permission to represent, or keep them for private reference."
  - *Before a share link is created (required checkbox, unchecked by default; nothing is stored or sent):* "☐ I own this brand, work for its owner, or have the owner's permission. I won't use this link to pass the kit off as the owner's official material."
  *Why:* conspicuous notice plus an affirmative act is what *Berman v. Freedom Financial Network*, 30 F.4th 849 (9th Cir. 2022) requires for online terms to bind. The checkbox also documents that the tool did not knowingly enable misuse (contributory-liability standard in *Inwood Labs v. Ives*, 456 U.S. 844 (1982); *Tiffany v. eBay*, 600 F.3d 93 (2d Cir. 2010)). *Source:* https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/20-16900/20-16900-2022-04-05.html . [Likely]
- **L-4 Keep the tool's own branding visually separate from kit content.** {Tool} chrome must not frame the kit as {Tool}'s own product page. *Why:* in the EU, an operator can be deemed to "use" a third-party sign when users perceive it as part of the operator's own commercial communication (CJEU *Louboutin v Amazon*, C-148/21 & C-184/21, 22 Dec 2022; contrast *Coty v Amazon*, C-567/18). *Source:* https://www.ippt.eu/sites/ippt/files/2022/IPPT20221222_CJEU_Louboutin_v_Amazon.pdf . [Likely]
- **L-5 Demos, placeholders, screenshots, and the portfolio case study use only the owner's own brands, fictional brands, or brands with written permission.** No `nike.com` placeholder, no "See Stripe's kit" gallery. *Why:* a demo of a real third-party brand is the operator's *own* use in promotion, which takes it outside the user-directed, nominative posture. [Likely]
- **L-6 Clear the tool's own name before launch.** Run a free USPTO trademark search (https://tmsearch.uspto.gov) and a web search, and avoid names close to the competitor or established brand tools. Federal registration costs money and is not needed; common-law rights come from use. [Likely]

### S. Share links and anti-impersonation

Threat: anyone can build a kit named "PayPal" with PayPal's logo and colors and send `https://{host}/k/#…` as "official brand guidelines". Phishing and fraud are the realistic harms. The server never sees the fragment, so every control below runs client-side or in static config.

- **S-1 Serve the share viewer on its own path ending in a slash, separate from the generator** (for example `/k/`). *Why:* Safe Browsing path-prefix expressions mean Google can list `{host}/k/` alone, without taking down `{host}/`. *Source:* https://developers.google.com/safe-browsing/v4/urls-hashing . [Certain on mechanics; Guessing on how Google scopes listings]
- **S-2 Consider a separate hostname for the viewer** (see §0.1). [Guessing; owner decision]
- **S-3 Persistent, non-dismissable banner** at the top of the share view, kept in print CSS and in any PNG/PDF exported from the share view:
  > **Unverified.** Someone made this kit with {Tool} and shared it as a link. {Tool} doesn't check who made it. It is not from, approved by, or connected to "{name}". This page never asks for logins, payments, or personal details. [Report this link]
  [Likely]
- **S-4 Page chrome never borrows the brand.** `document.title` = "Unverified brand kit · {Tool}". The kit name may follow, but never comes first. The favicon is always {Tool}'s own and never the kit's logo or favicon. Open Graph tags are static and generic (the server can't see the fragment anyway): "A brand kit made with {Tool}. Contents unverified." [Likely]
- **S-5 No interactive surface.** No `<form>`, `<input>`, `<textarea>`, or contenteditable on the share route. Headers on `/k/*`:
  `Content-Security-Policy: default-src 'self'; img-src 'self' data: blob:; font-src 'self' {font-cdn}; connect-src 'self'; form-action 'none'; frame-ancestors 'none'; base-uri 'none'; object-src 'none'`
  `X-Frame-Options: DENY`, `Referrer-Policy: no-referrer`, `X-Robots-Tag: noindex, nofollow, noarchive`, plus `<meta name="robots" content="noindex, nofollow, noarchive">`. *Why:* `form-action 'none'` and the absence of inputs make credential harvesting on {host} impossible. `frame-ancestors 'none'` stops a phishing page from framing {host} to borrow its URL bar. noindex keeps the viewer out of search. Browsers never send the fragment in `Referer` (RFC 9110 §10.1.3). *Sources:* https://www.w3.org/TR/CSP3/ ; https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#field.referer . [Certain on header behavior]
- **S-6 No clickable links from kit data.** Render source domains as plain text. Strip URLs, email addresses, and phone numbers from name and tagline (regex for schemes, `www.`, TLD patterns, `@`, digit runs ≥ 7). No "Visit site", "Log in", "Download official assets", or QR codes. [Likely]
- **S-7 Tight limits on free text and payload.** Name ≤ 60 chars, tagline ≤ 120 chars, ≤ 8 colors, ≤ 2 font families (names from the Google Fonts list or the detected list only), no notes or custom-section fields. Logo: **SVG only, sanitized** (DOMPurify SVG profile; drop `<script>`, `<foreignObject>`, `<image>`, event handlers, external `href`s, CSS `url()`), ≤ ~8 KB after sanitization. Render it via `<img src="data:image/svg+xml,…">` so scripts can't run. **Never put raster data in a share link.** *Why:* this limits phishing copy, injection, and illegal-image smuggling, which also matters for the UK OSA (§2.6). [Likely; size cap is Guessing]
- **S-8 Static blocklist checked before rendering.** `/blocklist.json` is served from the repo and holds (a) SHA-256 of normalized reported payloads and (b) normalized brand names and domains whose owners asked to block share links. On a match, show "This kit link has been disabled." and render nothing else. The generator also refuses to create share links for blocked names or domains. Updates ship through the normal free CI/CD deploy. *Why:* this is the only takedown lever when nothing is stored. Acting on specific knowledge is what keeps contributory trademark liability away (*Tiffany v. eBay*). [Likely]
- **S-9 Optional stronger interstitial for high-impersonation brands.** Keep a small list in the repo of banks, payment processors, large email and cloud providers, government agencies, and shipping carriers. Show a full-page "This page was made by an anonymous visitor…" click-through before rendering. [Guessing]
- **S-10 Optional expiry.** Encode the creation date in the payload and refuse to render links older than N months (for example 12). This shortens a phishing link's useful life but also breaks old legitimate links. [Guessing; owner decision]
- **S-11 Monitor the domain in Google Search Console** (free). A URL-prefix property for `{host}` can be verified by uploading an HTML file, with no DNS change. It shows Security Issues notices and the review-request flow if Safe Browsing ever flags the host. [Likely]

### R. Reporting and takedown (no PII collected by the tool)

- **R-1 Option A (simplest): `mailto:` report link** on the share banner and the Terms page. It opens the reporter's own mail app to a dedicated alias, with subject "Report: {Tool} share link" and the full URL (including `#…`) prefilled in the body. Also show the address as plain text. The tool collects nothing; the operator receives an email the reporter chose to send. Policy: use report emails only to act on the report, never add senders to any list, delete them within 30 days of resolution, and say so on the Terms/Privacy page. Add the line "Under 13? Ask a parent or guardian to report it." *Why:* this keeps the tool data-free and limits handling of incidental emails, including from children, in line with the owner's no-kids'-emails rule. [Guessing on the approach; Likely that a general-audience site with no actual knowledge is outside COPPA]
- **R-2 Option B (no reporter identity at all): one-tap report** → a small endpoint (behind the human check) that takes only `{payload, reason}`. Reason is one of: impersonates my brand, phishing/scam, copyright/trademark, other. There is **no free-text field**. Brand owners who want a permanent block still use email. [Guessing; check free-tier limits with the infra track]

## 2. Detailed findings

### 2.1 Fetching public websites on a user's request

**US federal: CFAA**

- *Van Buren v. United States*, 593 U.S. 374 (June 3, 2021). "Exceeds authorized access" means entering parts of a system one is not entitled to enter: "liability under both clauses stems from a gates-up-or-down inquiry—one either can or cannot access a computer system, and one either can or cannot access certain areas within the system." Footnote 8 leaves open whether the gates are only technical or also contractual. Source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-783_k53l.pdf [Certain]
- *hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn*, 31 F.4th 1180 (9th Cir. Apr. 18, 2022), on remand after *Van Buren*: for public pages, the "without authorization" concept doesn't apply because there is no gate to lift. But on Nov. 4, 2022 the district court held that LinkedIn's User Agreement bans on scraping and fake profiles were enforceable as contract. The case ended with a stipulated $500,000 judgment and a permanent injunction (Dec. 2022). The stipulated CFAA findings have no precedential value. Source: https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/12/08/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case/ [Certain]
  - *Lesson for the product:* the CFAA risk of fetching a public page is low. Contract and "technical gate" risk is where exposure lives. The tool never logs in, so it never accepts a site's terms (F-5).
- *Craigslist v. 3Taps*, 942 F. Supp. 2d 962 (N.D. Cal. 2013) held that continued access after a cease-and-desist plus an IP block was "without authorization". It predates *hiQ*, but it shows why the do-not-fetch list (F-6) and the no-bypass rule (F-5) matter. [Likely]
- *Ryanair v. Booking Holdings* (D. Del.): a July 2024 jury found CFAA liability ($5,000) for access that ran through a logged-in "myRyanair" area. On Jan. 22, 2025 Judge Bryson granted judgment as a matter of law because Ryanair failed to prove $5,000 of loss. An appeal was filed in 2025, and I found no appellate decision. Source: https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2025/03/court-overturns-a-bad-jury-verdict-against-scraping-ryanair-v-booking-guest-blog-post.htm ; https://www.rcfp.org/briefs-comments/ryanair-v-booking-com/ [Certain on the district court; appeal status unverified]

**US: contract (site terms of use)**

- *Meta Platforms v. Bright Data*, No. 3:23-cv-00077-EMC (N.D. Cal. Jan. 23, 2024, Judge Chen). Summary judgment for Bright Data: Meta's terms reached only logged-in use, so logged-off scraping of public data didn't breach them, and after account termination Meta couldn't bind Bright Data. Meta dropped the case the next month. Sources: https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/01/game-on-bright-data-scores-major-victory-in-web-scraping-dispute-with-meta-guest-blog-post.htm ; https://www.fbm.com/technology/publications/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-and-online-data-collection-meta-platforms-v-bright-data/ [Certain]
- *X Corp. v. Bright Data*, No. 3:23-cv-03698 (N.D. Cal., Judge Alsup). In May 2024 the contract claims based on copying public data were dismissed as conflict-preempted by the Copyright Act. Late in 2024 the court let X replead access claims based on server impairment. I found no later merits ruling. Sources: https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/05/district-court-adopts-broad-view ; https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2025/01/catching-up-on-the-heavyweight-scraping-battle-between-x-and-bright-data-guest-blog-post.htm [Certain through early 2025]
- *Southwest Airlines v. Kiwi.com*, No. 3:21-cv-00098 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 30, 2021). Preliminary injunction: browsewrap terms bound a scraper that had actual knowledge through cease-and-desist letters (following *BoardFirst*, same district), and the court rejected the argument that *hiQ* changed this. Kiwi dropped its Fifth Circuit appeal (Jan. 2022) and a permanent injunction followed a settlement. **This is the owner's home federal district.** Sources: https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2021/10/tos-supports-injunction-against-web-scraping-southwest-airlines-v-kiwi.htm ; https://manage.lawstreetmedia.com/?p=15231 [Certain on the holding; the appeal dismissal date is inferred]
- Browsewrap without notice generally doesn't bind (*Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble*, 763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014)). A single user-triggered fetch of a homepage almost never involves assent. Risk appears when the operator has *actual knowledge* of an objection, which F-6 handles. [Likely]

**Texas state law**

- Penal Code §33.02(a): "knowingly accesses a computer … without the effective consent of the owner." "Access" includes "retrieve … data from". §33.01(12): consent is not effective if "(A) induced by deception … or (E) used for a purpose other than that for which the consent was given." Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §143.001 gives a civil action to anyone "injured … as a result of a violation under Chapter 33" committed knowingly or intentionally, with actual damages and attorney's fees (§143.002). This purpose-based test is broader than *Van Buren*. No reported case applying it to a single public-page fetch was found. Honest identification (F-3) and the do-not-fetch list (F-6) are the practical defenses. Sources: https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.33.htm ; https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/CP/htm/CP.143.htm [Certain on text; Guessing on how a court would apply it]

**UK and EU**

- UK Computer Misuse Act 1990 s.1 requires *unauthorised* access. Public pages fetched without bypassing controls are authorised in practice. [Likely]
- **EU sui generis database right** (Directive 96/9/EC, Art. 7): the maker of a database with substantial investment can stop extraction or re-utilisation of a substantial part, or repeated and systematic extraction of insubstantial parts. *CV-Online Latvia v Melons* (C-762/19, 3 June 2021) requires that the extraction risk the maker's investment. A website's CSS and a handful of design tokens from one page are, at most, an insubstantial part of any "database", taken once at a user's request. The UK keeps an equivalent right. Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/1996/9/oj ; https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2021/uk/cv-online-latvia-cjeu-complicates-the-enforcement-of-database-rights [Likely: negligible risk]
- *Ryanair v PR Aviation* (C-30/14, 2015): where no database right applies, site terms can still restrict use contractually. That is the same as the US contract point and is handled the same way. [Likely]
- **EU TDM exception** (DSM Directive 2019/790 Art. 4): copies made for automated analysis of lawfully accessible works are permitted unless the rightsholder reserved rights "in an appropriate manner, such as machine-readable means". Whether a brand-token extractor counts as "TDM" is debatable. The temporary-copy exception (InfoSoc Art. 5(1)) probably covers it anyway. Honoring robots.txt (F-4) and TDMRep (F-10) removes the question. *Kneschke v. LAION*: LG Hamburg (Sept. 27, 2024) suggested a natural-language reservation could suffice. The OLG Hamburg (5 U 104/24, reported Dec. 10, 2025) dismissed the appeal, held the reservation at issue was not machine-readable, and admitted a further appeal to the BGH. Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/790/oj ; https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/germany/higher-regional-court-hamburg-confirms-ai-training-was-permitted-(kneschke-v,-d-,-laion) ; https://heise.de/-11111575 [Likely]

**robots.txt policy decision**

- Not legally required in the US. RFC 9309 says the rules "are not a form of access authorization". [Certain]
- Google's user-triggered fetchers "generally ignore robots.txt rules" because a user asked for the fetch, so ignoring it is defensible industry practice. [Certain]
- **Recommendation: honor it anyway** (F-4). The cost is one extra request per origin plus some sites becoming unanalyzable, which the upload fallback absorbs. The benefits are an EU TDM-opt-out answer, Texas good-faith evidence, a clean story if a brand complains, and alignment with "legally sound everywhere". [Guessing on the cost-benefit]
- Cloudflare-managed robots.txt files now often carry AI "content signals". {Tool} is not an AI crawler and does no training, so only the standard `Allow`/`Disallow` groups apply. [Likely]

**User-Agent**

- Recommended format: `Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; {Token}/1.0; +https://{host}/bot)`. The `compatible;` convention is the same pattern Googlebot and Google-Site-Verification use (`Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; Google-Site-Verification/1.0)`). It identifies the bot plainly and avoids naive "empty UA" blocks without pretending to be a browser. Source: https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-user-triggered-fetchers [Certain on the Google example]

### 2.2 Copyright

- **Colors and hex codes**: facts, and "mere variations of … coloring" are excluded by regulation (37 C.F.R. §202.1(a)). [Certain]
- **Font names**: names are not copyrightable (§202.1(a); Compendium §313.4(C) lists product names). [Certain] **Typeface designs** are not copyrightable in the US (§202.1(e)), but **font software** is protected as a computer program (Copyright Office 1992 policy; *Adobe Systems v. Southern Software*, N.D. Cal. 1998). So a font's *name* and *look* are free to describe, but its *file* is not free to copy. That is why T-1 and T-4 exist. The EU and UK protect typeface designs more broadly (e.g., the UK CDPA s.54–55), which is one more reason never to copy a font file. [Certain for the US rules; Likely for the cases and EU/UK]
- **CSS code**: can carry thin copyright as text or software. The tool extracts values and doesn't republish code, so this is low risk. [Likely]
- **Logos**: often only thinly protected. The Compendium §313.4(J) bars registering "familiar symbols and designs" and "a simple combination of a few familiar symbols". More elaborate logos are protected pictorial works. The *user* decides to put a logo in their own kit. The tool's server makes at most a transient copy at the user's direction, and the kit is assembled in the user's browser. That puts the operator closer to a tool provider (*Sony*) than a publisher. Source: https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/ [Likely]
- **Images on scanned pages** (og:image, hero photos): only fetch them as logo candidates under the F-1 caps. Treat og:image as a last resort, loaded only when the user picks it, because it is often a photograph with its own rights. [Guessing]
- **Generated kits**: the template layout is the owner's copyrighted work, licensed to users per ToS §8. User inputs stay the user's. [Likely]

### 2.3 Trademark

- **The operator's use.** The operator does not use third-party marks as its own source identifier. The tool displays them at the user's direction in the user's own document. US nominative fair use (*New Kids*, 9th Cir. 1992; *Toyota v. Tabari*, 610 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2010)). The **Fifth Circuit** treats nominative use as part of likelihood of confusion, needing only two of the three *New Kids* factors (*Pebble Beach v. Tour 18*, 155 F.3d 526 (5th Cir. 1998)), and found use beyond nominative where it suggested sponsorship (*Bd. of Supervisors LSU v. Smack Apparel*, 550 F.3d 465 (5th Cir. 2008)). Source (secondary): https://nick-guinn.squarespace.com/blog/we-always-beat-xs-prices-a-look-at-nominative-fair-use-in-the-fifth-circuit [Likely]
- **Contributory liability** requires intentional inducement, or continued supply to someone the provider knows or has reason to know is infringing (*Inwood*). For services, it requires specific knowledge (*Tiffany v. eBay*). The blocklist (S-8) is how the operator shows it acts on specific knowledge. [Certain on doctrine]
- **Single colors can be trademarks** (*Qualitex v. Jacobson*, 514 U.S. 159 (1995)), for example Tiffany blue or T-Mobile magenta. Listing a hex value is not trademark use. A *user* who adopts a famous brand's color for a competing brand takes their own risk, which ToS §6 and §7 cover. [Certain on Qualitex; Likely on application]
- **EU.** EUTMR (Reg. 2017/1001) Art. 14(1)(c) allows referential use "in accordance with honest practices" (Art. 14(2)). *Louboutin v Amazon* (CJEU Grand Chamber, 22 Dec 2022) says an operator can be treated as *using* a sign when users perceive it as part of the operator's own commercial communication, hence L-4. Private, non-trade use by end users is outside infringement in the EU ("use in the course of trade"). Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/1001/oj ; https://www.ippt.eu/sites/ippt/files/2022/IPPT20221222_CJEU_Louboutin_v_Amazon.pdf [Likely]
- **Disclaimer wording** (used in L-2, S-3, ToS §7): "not affiliated with or endorsed by {Brand}". Disclaimers help but don't cure a confusing presentation (*Pebble Beach*), so the S-* design controls carry the weight. [Likely]
- **Section 230 does not cover IP claims.** 47 U.S.C. §230(e)(2) says it doesn't "limit or expand any law pertaining to intellectual property". Federal trademark and copyright claims are outside §230. Circuits split on state IP (*Perfect 10 v. CCBill*, 9th Cir. 2007, vs. *Hepp v. Facebook*, 3d Cir. 2021). Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230 ; https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2021/09/third-circuit-says-section-230-doesnt-apply-to-publicity-rights-claims-hepp-v-facebook.htm [Certain]

### 2.4 Impersonation and phishing abuse

- **Threat model.** Share links render a polished, credible brand page on a domain the attacker doesn't control. Typical uses: fake "brand partner onboarding" or "vendor portal" pages that point victims elsewhere, credibility props in invoice and BEC fraud, and fake "press kit" pages. The server never sees the content, so detection is report-driven only. [Likely]
- **FTC Impersonation Rule** (16 C.F.R. Part 461, effective April 1, 2024) bans impersonating government and businesses in commerce. The Feb. 2024 supplemental proposal would have added "means and instrumentalities" liability for providers who know or have reason to know their goods or services will be used for impersonation. In Dec. 2024 the FTC said it would **not proceed with that provision "at this time"** but would keep considering it, and held an informal hearing on Jan. 17, 2025 about extending the rule to impersonating individuals. I found no final action after that. **If means-and-instrumentalities is ever adopted, the S-* and R-* controls are the evidence that the operator had no reason to know of misuse.** Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-461 ; https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-12-26/pdf/2024-30718.pdf [Certain through Jan 2025; status after that unverified]
- **Safe Browsing mechanics** (§0.1, S-1, S-2, S-11): fragments are removed; up to 5 host strings (the exact host plus parents up to the last five components) and 6 path strings are checked. [Certain] Source: https://developers.google.com/safe-browsing/v4/urls-hashing
- **Why each control exists:**
  - noindex: no SEO amplification of phishing kits.
  - banner and title: a victim sees "Unverified" first.
  - no forms plus `form-action 'none'`: credentials can't be harvested on {host}.
  - no links: the page can't be a hop to a phishing site.
  - text limits and URL/email/phone stripping: no room for scam instructions.
  - SVG-only sanitized logos: no script or raster smuggling.
  - `frame-ancestors 'none'`: attackers can't frame {host}.
  - blocklist and reports: a takedown lever.
  - generic OG tags: link previews in Slack and email never show the brand.

  [Likely]
- **Self-hosted forks.** With MIT, an attacker could host the viewer without banners. That doesn't touch {host} or the owner's reputation, and attackers can build such pages without the code anyway. Not a reason to withhold an open license. [Likely]

### 2.5 Font licensing (detail)

- **SIL OFL 1.1 (most Google Fonts):** embedding in PDFs is allowed in full or subset (FAQ 1.12). The document's license is unaffected (1.13). No license text is needed inside a document (1.10). Hosting font files for others requires the full package with its license (1.8). WOFF/WOFF2 conversion counts as unmodified only when the data is unchanged and metadata is preserved (2.2.1). Subsetting webfonts is modification and drops RFN use (2.6). PDF embedding is not "webfont" use (2.10). Source: https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/ [Certain]
- **Apache-2.0 and Ubuntu Font Licence fonts on Google Fonts:** Google states all its fonts are open source and usable commercially "on any surface". Check each family's license at build time and record it on `/credits`. Source: https://developers.google.com/fonts/faq [Certain on Google's statement; Likely that UFL embedding is unrestricted]
- **Adobe Fonts:** licensed only to subscribers. Desktop-font PDF embedding is allowed *for the subscriber*, subset and protected (§3.1(A)). Self-hosting, redistributing, enabling others' use, and copying fonts out of Web Projects are all prohibited (§3.4(E)(1)–(3), (7)). The tool has no subscription and so no rights. Source: Adobe Fonts Product Specific Terms (2024-06-18), linked in T-4 [Certain]
- **Commercial self-hosted fonts found on scanned sites:** web-font EULAs typically license a specific domain or pageview tier. Downloading the file and embedding it into a user's PDF would be an unlicensed reproduction and distribution of font software, and possibly a breach of the site owner's own EULA terms that the tool would be inducing. **Do not fetch them.** Detect, label, and substitute (T-5). [Likely]
- **fsType** is the font's own embedding flag (T-3). Honor it even for open fonts, because a few legacy fonts carry restrictive bits despite open licenses. OFL-FAQ 4.2.2 tells authors to remove contradictory fsType values. [Certain]
- **Font delivery services:** Google Fonts (`fonts.gstatic.com`; API "does not set or log cookies", but IPs reach Google); Bunny Fonts (claims zero logging; Google Fonts v1 API-compatible); Fontsource (npm packages, mirrored by jsDelivr). The legal license of the font files is the same whichever service delivers them. The choice is a privacy and infra question. [Likely]

### 2.6 DMCA §512, Section 230, DSA, UK Online Safety Act

- **17 U.S.C. §512(c)** protects service providers against liability "by reason of the storage at the direction of a user of material that resides on a system or network controlled or operated by or for the service provider", and requires a designated agent registered with the Copyright Office (§512(c)(2)). **{Tool} stores nothing.** Share data lives in the URL fragment, which browsers never send to the server. Uploads never leave the browser. So §512(c) has nothing to protect, and the designated agent buys nothing. The fetcher's transient handling of third-party pages is closest to §512(a)/(b) (transmission and caching), but those also assume no modification or selection. The real protection is the low-risk design, not a safe harbor. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512 [Certain on statute; Likely on application]
- **Designated agent fee:** $6 per designation, amendment, or resubmission, renewed every 3 years. **This conflicts with the $0 rule; don't register unless kits are ever stored.** Still publish a copyright/trademark contact voluntarily (R-1). Sources: https://www.copyright.gov/rulemaking/onlinesp/NPR/faq.html ; https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/ [Likely: fee from the Copyright Office FAQ and secondary sources; the directory page didn't render a fee statement]
- **47 U.S.C. §230(c)(1)** could shield the operator from non-IP state claims (for example, defamation in a tagline) over user-supplied share content, since it is "information provided by another information content provider". It does nothing for IP claims (§230(e)(2)) or federal criminal law, and it doesn't travel outside the US. Content the tool *itself* generates (extracted palettes, generated rules) is the operator's own, not third-party content. Relevance is marginal. [Certain on text; Likely on application]
- **EU Digital Services Act (Reg. 2022/2065).** "Intermediary service" means mere conduit, caching, or hosting (Art. 3(g)). With no storage, {Tool} is not a hosting service. The fetcher retrieves information for the user and returns derived results; it doesn't transmit information *provided by* the user, so "mere conduit" is a poor fit. If {Tool} were an intermediary, a non-EU provider must designate an EU legal representative in writing (Art. 13), and hosting adds notice-and-action (Art. 16) and statements of reasons (Art. 17). Those are paid obligations. **Fragment-only sharing keeps the tool out of this.** Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj ; https://www.bipt.be/operators/publication/providers-of-intermediary-services-without-an-establishment-in-the-eu-appointment-of-a-legal-representative [Likely; attorney check]
- **UK Online Safety Act 2023** s.3(1) defines a user-to-user service as one "by means of which content that is generated directly on the service by a user of the service, or uploaded to or shared on the service by a user of the service, may be encountered by another user". Storage is not an element. A share link arguably fits: user A generates a kit on the service and user B encounters it on the service. The counterargument is that user B supplies the content in their own URL, so the service only renders B's input. The Act regulates services with "links with the UK" (a significant number of UK users, the UK as a target market, or a material risk of significant harm to UK individuals, s.4). Ofcom does not define "significant number". If in scope, the duties for a small, low-risk service are: a written illegal-content risk assessment (fraud is a priority offence), a named responsible person, a way to take content down (the blocklist), a reporting and complaints route (R-*), clear terms, and a children's access assessment. **Recommendation:** write the risk assessment (Ofcom's free tool; a few hours; keep it in the repo or private) as cheap insurance, or drop share links for UK users (not technically clean). Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/3 ; https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/information-for-industry/illegal-harms/overview-of-regulated-services.pdf [Guessing on applicability; Certain on the definition text]
- **Texas SCOPE Act (HB 18)** reaches digital services that let users socially interact *and* create profiles or post content. {Tool} has no accounts, profiles, or posting, so it is out of scope. Parts of the Act are also enjoined (W.D. Tex. 2024–2025). Source: https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/district-court-blocks-enforcement-of-scope-act-requirements [Likely]

### 2.7 Terms of Service: enforceability notes

- **Formation without accounts.** Under *Berman* (9th Cir. 2022), terms bind only with (1) reasonably conspicuous notice and (2) an action that unambiguously shows assent. Gray, small, low-contrast links failed in *Berman*. So the L-3 notice must be readable (body size, real link styling, adjacent to the action), and the share checkbox is the strongest assent point. Texas courts apply similar notice principles. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/20-16900/20-16900-2022-04-05.html [Certain on Berman; Likely on Texas]
- **Free service and consumer law.** The EU Digital Content Directive (2019/770) applies where the consumer pays a price or provides personal data. Processing data only to supply the service is excluded (Art. 3(1)), so EU statutory conformity remedies likely don't attach to a free, data-free tool. The UK Consumer Rights Act 2015 quality rights for digital content apply to content "supplied for a price" (s.33). The Texas DTPA defines a "consumer" as someone who acquires goods or services "by purchase or lease" (§17.45(4)), which a free user doesn't do. Mandatory rules still limit exclusions (ToS §4 carve-out). Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/770/oj ; https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/33 [Likely]
- **Texas fair-notice rules** (*Dresser*, 1993): risk-shifting clauses (releases, indemnities, exculpatory clauses) need express language and conspicuousness. Format the disclaimer and liability cap in bold or all caps under a clear heading. [Certain on Dresser]
- **Personal liability.** As a sole proprietor, the owner has no entity shield. The ToS cap, the no-storage design, and the abuse controls are the protection. Forming an LLC costs money and is outside the locked decisions, so it's listed for attorney discussion only. [Certain that sole proprietors are personally liable]
- **Venue.** Texas state courts in the owner's county, or N.D. Tex., Dallas Division. EU/UK consumers keep home-court rights (Brussels Ia Art. 18). Business users can generally be bound by the forum clause (Brussels Ia Art. 25). [Likely]
